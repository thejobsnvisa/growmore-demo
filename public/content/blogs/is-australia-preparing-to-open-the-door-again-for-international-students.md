Australia’s Student visa program has undergone significant change over the past few years. While Student visas have not formally been “closed”, offshore applicants from a number of countries and education sectors have experienced substantially greater scrutiny and higher refusal rates. Against this background, the Government’s recent announcements raise an important question: **could Australia be preparing for a new phase of international student migration?**

On 17 September 2026, Minister for Home Affairs Tony Burke announced proposed changes affecting international students and Temporary Graduate visa holders, including significant restrictions on the ability of many future visa holders to bring family members to Australia. Subject to the final details and implementation of these changes, exceptions are expected to apply to certain cohorts. From a migration planning perspective, this is an important development because international student numbers and Net Overseas Migration (NOM) are not necessarily the same thing.

For example, if 100,000 primary Student visa holders enter Australia and a substantial proportion are accompanied by partners and children, the overall contribution to NOM can be significantly greater than 100,000 people. If future policy limits the number of secondary applicants accompanying students, theoretically the Government could accommodate a greater number of genuine primary students without producing the same level of overall population growth. This raises an interesting possibility:**could restricting student dependants ultimately create more capacity for genuine primary Student visa applicants?**

At this stage, there has been no Government announcement confirming that Student visa requirements will be relaxed or that offshore Student visa grants will substantially increase. This is therefore an observation and interpretation of the recent policy direction, rather than confirmed Government policy.

The broader economic and education context is also important. Recent ABC commentary highlighted the significant contribution international students make to Australian universities. International student revenue supports not only the education of overseas students but also university teaching, research and the broader tertiary education system. Australia therefore faces a difficult policy balance between managing migration levels and maintaining a strong and financially sustainable international education sector. International students remain an important part of Australia’s education system and economy.

Another significant development from Minister Burke’s 17 September announcement relates to skilled migration and trades. The Government has indicated that specified housing trade qualifications will receive equivalent recognition to university qualifications under proposed changes to the skilled migration points system. This is particularly interesting because Australia continues to face shortages in construction and housing-related occupations, while offshore access to vocational and trade study pathways has become extremely difficult for applicants from a number of countries.

This creates another important policy question. If Australia wants to attract and retain more qualified tradespeople, could we eventually see improved opportunities for genuine international students seeking to undertake trade qualifications in Australia? There has been no announcement confirming such a change, but the Government’s increasing emphasis on trades, housing and skills makes this an area worth closely monitoring.

Importantly, during his National Press Club appearance, Minister Burke also indicated that the Government’s international student planning levels had not changed. At the same time, the Government continues to emphasise stronger integrity measures, including closer examination of financial capacity and whether an applicant is genuinely seeking to study in Australia. This may indicate that the longer-term policy objective is not simply to reduce international education, but rather to better control **who enters the Student visa program and how that program contributes to overall migration numbers.**

So, is Australia about to “reopen” Student visas? **There is currently no confirmed announcement to that effect.** Student visas have remained legally available, although obtaining a visa has become considerably more difficult for certain applicants and sectors. Nevertheless, when we consider the proposed restrictions on dependants, unchanged student planning levels, the economic importance of international education and the Government’s renewed focus on trade skills, there are reasonable grounds to closely watch what happens next.

One possible future direction could be a Student visa program that facilitates more genuine primary students while restricting accompanying dependants and therefore managing the overall impact on Net Overseas Migration. Whether that is ultimately the Government’s intention remains to be seen.

For students and education providers, the important message is to **watch this space**. These developments should not presently be interpreted as confirmation that Student visa settings are being relaxed. However, they may indicate an important shift in the way the Government intends to balance international education, skilled migration and population growth.
