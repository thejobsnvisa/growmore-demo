*An opinion by a Registered Migration Agent*

For many years, Australia's skilled migration program has revolved around one fundamental question:

**Is my occupation on the occupation list?**

This single question has influenced career choices, university enrolments, migration planning and financial decisions for hundreds of thousands of prospective migrants.

Traditionally, the answer was relatively straightforward. If your occupation appeared on the Medium and Long-term Strategic Skills List (MLTSSL), the Short-term Skilled Occupation List (STSOL) or the Regional Occupation List (ROL), you could reasonably expect that, provided you met the relevant visa requirements and remained competitive, there would be an opportunity to pursue skilled migration.

Today, however, that assumption deserves to be challenged.

## The Occupation Lists No Longer Reflect Migration Outcomes

As migration professionals, we regularly advise clients who believe that the presence of their occupation on an official occupation list is evidence that Australia is actively seeking migrants in that occupation. Unfortunately, this is increasingly not the case.

Across many occupations listed on the MLTSSL, STSOL and ROL, invitation activity through the General Skilled Migration program has become extremely limited or, in some cases, virtually non-existent.

While occupations remain officially eligible, the practical opportunity to receive an invitation has significantly diminished. This creates a growing disconnect between government policy documents and the actual operation of Australia's skilled migration program.

## Eligibility Is Not the Same as Opportunity

One of the biggest misconceptions in Australian migration today is the belief that eligibility automatically translates into a realistic migration pathway.

It does not.

Being listed on an occupation list simply establishes that an occupation may be eligible for particular visa subclasses. It does not indicate the likelihood of receiving an invitation, the level of competition, the planning priorities of individual states or the practical demand within the migration program. Unfortunately, this distinction is often overlooked by applicants, education providers and even employers.

The result is that many prospective migrants invest years of study, substantial financial resources and considerable personal sacrifice pursuing occupations that, although technically eligible, have little realistic prospect of invitation under current settings.

## A List Must Serve a Practical Purpose

Every policy instrument should have a clear purpose.

Historically, Australia's occupation lists were designed to identify occupations experiencing genuine skills shortages and to guide skilled migration accordingly. If that remains their purpose, an obvious question arises.

Why do so many occupations remain on these lists when invitation activity for those occupations has been negligible for extended periods?

If an occupation is consistently not being invited, should it continue to be presented as part of Australia's skilled migration framework?

Conversely, if Australia's labour market genuinely requires workers in a particular occupation, should there not be a migration pathway that reflects that demand? These are legitimate policy questions that deserve greater public discussion.

## Greater Transparency Is Needed

Migration is one of the most significant financial and personal decisions an individual can make. Applicants deserve more than a simple confirmation that their occupation appears on a published list.

They deserve transparency regarding whether that occupation currently has realistic migration prospects. A modern migration system should provide clearer information

Providing this information would allow applicants to make informed decisions based on realistic expectations rather than assumptions.

## Time for a Policy Review

This is not an argument that every occupation should receive invitations every year. Migration planning must remain responsive to economic conditions, workforce shortages and national priorities.

However, if Australia's migration program has fundamentally shifted away from relying on traditional occupation lists, then policy settings should reflect that reality.

Maintaining lengthy occupation lists that no longer align with invitation outcomes risks creating confusion rather than certainty.

As migration advisers, our role is increasingly shifting from helping clients determine whether their occupation is on the list to explaining why, despite being on the list, they may still have little or no practical pathway to permanent residence. That, in itself, suggests the current framework warrants review.

## Conclusion

Australia's occupation lists have been the foundation of the skilled migration program for many years. However, a policy tool is only effective if it reflects the reality of how the migration program operates.

If occupations remain on the list but have little or no realistic prospect of invitation, the lists risk creating false expectations rather than providing meaningful guidance.

It is time for a comprehensive review of Australia's skilled occupation lists. In my view, four reforms should be considered:

1. Restore a regular and predictable invitation program for the General Skilled Migration stream, similar to the approach used before COVID-19. Consistent invitation rounds for both the Skilled Independent (subclass 189) and State/Territory nominated pathways Subclass 190 & 491) would provide greater certainty for applicants, improve workforce planning, and reduce the growing backlog of Expressions of Interest.

2. Review and modernise the occupation lists so they accurately reflect the current objectives of Australia's skilled migration program and Australia's workforce needs.

3. Retain only those occupations that have a genuine and realistic pathway to invitation. If an occupation remains on the list, applicants should reasonably expect that, where they are competitive, there is a realistic opportunity to receive an invitation.

4. If the occupation lists can no longer fulfil that purpose, replace the current model with a more transparent and contemporary framework that accurately reflects Australia's migration priorities rather than maintaining lists that no longer align with migration outcomes.

Australia's migration system has earned a strong international reputation for integrity and transparency. The occupation lists should continue to support that reputation by providing clear, reliable and meaningful guidance to prospective skilled migrants, employers and migration professionals alike.
