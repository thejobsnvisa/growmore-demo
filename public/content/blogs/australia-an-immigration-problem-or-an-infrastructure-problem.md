As migration professionals, we regularly hear one question: **Is Australia bringing in too many migrants?**

However, after reviewing recent reporting by *The Sydney Morning Herald* and *The Age*, we believe the more relevant question for Australia's migration program is whether the country has adequately planned for the migrants it already relies upon.

In a recent *Morning Edition* discussion, investigative journalist **Eryk Bagshaw** examined Australia's increasing reliance on temporary migrants and the infrastructure pressures being experienced in rapidly growing parts of the country. His conclusion was that Australia's challenge should not simply be characterised as an immigration problem; infrastructure and long-term population planning are also central to the issue.

From a migration perspective, this is an important distinction.

## Permanent Migration Is Only Part of the Picture

When discussing Australia's migration numbers, it is important to distinguish between the **Permanent Migration Program, temporary visa holders and Net Overseas Migration (NOM)**.

As Bagshaw's reporting highlights, Australia's permanent migration intake has remained relatively stable over the longer term, while the temporary migrant population has grown significantly. Temporary migrants include international students, Working Holiday Makers and employer-sponsored workers, among others.

This is important because Australia's migration system cannot simply be reduced to one headline number.

Temporary visa holders now perform an important role across Australian industries, including agriculture, hospitality, education and skilled employment. Any significant reduction in these visa programs may therefore have consequences for employers and industries that depend upon these workers.

## Australia's Migration Needs Are Different Across the Country

Another important point from the reporting is the significant difference between metropolitan and regional Australia.

In some rapidly growing metropolitan areas, population growth is placing additional pressure on housing, transport, schools and other infrastructure.

At the same time, many regional communities are facing a completely different challenge — **an ageing population, difficulty attracting workers and declining local businesses**.

Bagshaw's reporting refers to regional communities where farmers and businesses are actively seeking younger workers and families to settle locally.

For migration policy, this raises an important consideration.

The question should perhaps not simply be **how many migrants Australia accepts**, but also **which migrants Australia needs, where those migrants are needed, and whether appropriate employment and infrastructure exist in those locations**.

## Skilled Migration Remains Important to Australia

From our experience working with employers and skilled migrants, Australia's dependence on skilled migration remains evident.

Employer-sponsored migration exists because businesses can face genuine difficulty sourcing suitably skilled workers within Australia. Regional migration programs similarly attempt to encourage skilled workers to live and work outside the major metropolitan centres.

At the same time, Australia's skilled migration settings have increasingly focused on salary levels, genuine skilled positions and appropriate employment conditions.

This indicates that the migration debate is becoming less about simply increasing or reducing migration numbers and more about **better targeting Australia's migration intake towards genuine economic and workforce requirements**.

## Migration Cannot Be Considered Separately From Infrastructure

Migration undoubtedly contributes to population growth and therefore increases demand for housing and public services.

However, migration policy alone does not determine how quickly Australia builds houses, roads, hospitals, schools or public transport.

The reporting highlights Melbourne's outer growth areas as an example where residential development has occurred rapidly while supporting infrastructure has struggled to keep pace.

From a migration-policy perspective, this is significant. If Australia continues to require skilled workers, international students and temporary workers, **migration planning must operate alongside housing, infrastructure and regional-development planning**.

## What Does This Mean for Migrants?

For prospective migrants, the current public debate should not automatically be interpreted as meaning that Australia no longer requires migration.

Rather, we are seeing an increasingly targeted migration environment.

An applicant's **occupation, skills, salary, employer, work experience, location and individual circumstances** are becoming increasingly important in determining available migration pathways.

For this reason, applicants should be cautious about reacting to broad headlines such as *"Australia is cutting migration"* or *"Australia has too many migrants"*. These statements do not necessarily reflect what is happening within a particular skilled, employer-sponsored or regional visa pathway.

## Our View as Migration Professionals

The recent reporting raises an important issue for Australia's future migration policy.

Australia needs to determine not only **how many migrants it requires**, but also **what skills are required, where those skills are required, and whether housing and infrastructure can support population growth in those locations**.

Migration can assist Australia with genuine workforce shortages and regional population challenges. However, effective migration policy must be accompanied by appropriate infrastructure and long-term population planning.

For migrants and employers, the focus should therefore remain on understanding how their individual circumstances fit within Australia's increasingly targeted migration system — rather than making decisions based solely on the broader political debate surrounding migration.
