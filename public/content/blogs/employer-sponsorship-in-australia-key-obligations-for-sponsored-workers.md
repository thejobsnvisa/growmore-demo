Sponsoring an overseas worker in Australia is not just about obtaining approval and nominating a suitable employee. It comes with a range of employer sponsorship obligations that employers need to meet even after becoming an approved sponsor. These responsibilities are designed to ensure sponsored workers are employed in the nominated occupation, receive appropriate employment conditions and are treated in accordance with Australian workplace and immigration requirements.

If an Australian business fails to meet any of these, it may result in compliance action, financial penalties and restrictions on future sponsorship.

So, let’s understand these obligations in this guide, which can help employers manage their sponsorship responsibilities while helping sponsored workers understand their rights and responsibilities.

## 1. Report Changes to the Department of Home Affairs

One of the key responsibilities of an Australian sponsor is notifying the Department of Home Affairs when certain changes occur after sponsorship approval or during the sponsorship period. For example, employers must report relevant changes involving:

- Business name, trading name or registration details
- Business structure, address or communication contact
- Owners, directors, principals or partners
- Insolvency, bankruptcy, liquidation, administration or closure
- A sponsored worker who does not commence employment
- A sponsored worker whose employment ends
- Changes to the sponsored worker’s duties

Employers are generally given 28 calendar days to notify of any of these specified events through ImmiAccount or through the relevant sponsor notification channel.

## 2. Ensure the Sponsored Worker Performs the Nominated Occupation

A sponsored worker must work in the occupation for which they were nominated.

This means an employer cannot simply move a sponsored employee into a substantially different occupation without considering whether a new nomination or other immigration action is required.

For example, if a worker was nominated for one occupation but the employer later wants them to perform duties belonging to another occupation, the sponsorship arrangements should be reviewed before the change is implemented.

For Skills in Demand visa (subclass 482) and [Skilled Employer Sponsored Regional (Provisional) visa (subclass 494) holders](https://www.growmore.one/our-services/employers/regional-certifying-body-approval/), additional visa requirements can also apply when changing occupation or sponsor.

## 3. Provide Appropriate Employment Conditions

Employer sponsorship does not allow a business to offer a sponsored worker less favourable conditions than an equivalent Australian worker.

For a [Standard Business Sponsor](https://www.growmore.one/our-services/employers/standard-business-sponsorship/), where the sponsored worker’s annual earnings are below AUD 250,000, the employer must ensure that the worker receives at least the annual earnings specified in the approved nomination.

This obligation generally begins when the relevant visa is granted, or when the nomination is approved if the worker already holds the relevant visa. It continues until the worker stops working for the sponsor or is granted another substantive visa that takes effect.

## 4. Do Not Discriminate in Recruitment

Standard Business Sponsors operating lawfully in Australia must not engage in discriminatory recruitment practices that adversely affect Australian citizens or other people based on visa or citizenship status.

Sponsors should also retain records demonstrating that recruitment processes did not discriminate on these grounds.

## 5. Keep Proper Sponsorship Records

Record-keeping is an important part of sponsorship compliance in Australia. Sponsors must keep records demonstrating compliance with their obligations.

Depending on the circumstances, these can include:

- Written employment contracts
- Duties performed by the sponsored worker
- Where the work was performed
- Earnings paid to the worker
- Non-monetary benefits
- Conditions of an equivalent Australian worker
- Travel-cost requests and payments
- Notifications made to Home Affairs
- Relevant training obligation records
- Other records required under a work agreement

So, if a departmental officer requests information, the sponsor must provide the relevant records or information in the required manner and within the specified timeframe.

## 6. Employers Must Bear Certain Sponsorship and Recruitment Costs

Sponsors are responsible for certain costs related to sponsoring and recruiting an overseas worker. These costs must not be passed on to the sponsored worker or their family members.

This may include costs of becoming an approved sponsor, nomination application charges, migration agent fees for sponsorship or nomination, recruitment agent fees, advertising and recruitment costs, candidate screening and shortlisting, certain background checks, recruitment and HR staff costs, and other specified recruitment expenses.

## What Happens If an Employer Breaches Sponsorship Obligations?

Failing to meet sponsorship obligations can have serious consequences. Depending on the circumstances, authorities may:

- Issue a compliance notice
- Bar an employer from sponsoring additional workers
- Refuse future sponsorship applications
- Cancel existing sponsorship approvals
- Require an enforceable undertaking
- Issue an infringement notice
- Seek civil penalties through the courts
- Apply an employer prohibition in serious cases

The current Department guidance states that an infringement notice can be up to AUD 15,840 for an individual and AUD 79,200 for a body corporate for each obligation breach. Civil penalties through the courts can be higher, including up to AUD 396,000 for a corporation for each failure.
